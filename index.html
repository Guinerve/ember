1

How a fire heats fuel it hasn’t touched yet

A flame front is a hot, glowing surface. Everything in front of it receives radiant heat, and how much depends on two things: how hot the flame is, raised to the fourth power, and the angles at which the flame and the fuel face each other.

Stefan–Boltzmann: why temperature dominates

Every surface radiates. A perfect emitter at absolute temperature T gives off σT⁴ watts per square metre. Real flames are slightly less efficient, so the emitted power carries an emissivity ε between 0 and 1. The fourth power is the striking part: a flame at 1,200 K radiates about 47% more than one at 1,090 K, though it is only 10% hotter.

The flame colour on this page is computed from Planck’s law for the temperature you set, so it shows the temperature in the colour that temperature really produces. Most of the energy is invisible: a 1,090 K flame emits most strongly near 2.7 µm, in the infrared.

The view factor: angles, not just distance

Only part of what the flame emits lands on any one leaf. The fraction is the view factor F, which adds up every small piece of flame weighted by two cosines. θ₁ is how far the ray leaves the flame’s face from square-on, and θ₂ is how far it meets the leaf from square-on. Then divide by πr².

Lean the flame forward and watch the ground under it brighten. Wind does this to real fires: the flame tilts over unburnt fuel, both cosines grow, distances shrink, and the fuel ahead receives far more heat. Turn the leaf from facing the sky to facing the fire and its flux changes too, though nothing else has moved.

Flames are modelled as flat rectangular radiating panels, the idealisation used by AS 3959 for building in bushfire-prone areas. That standard assumes a 1,090 K flame. Its bushfire attack levels are radiant-flux bands at 12.5, 19, 29 and 40 kW/m². No atmospheric absorption is included, so fluxes far from the flame are slightly high.

2

Radiation, convection, conduction

Heat reaches unburnt fuel three ways. They act at very different ranges, and which one matters depends on how far ahead of the flame you look.

Radiation travels at the speed of light, needs no contact, and is the only path that reaches metres ahead. It preheats and dries fuel long before the flame arrives.

Convection is heat carried by moving hot gas. It needs contact. Upright flames send their hot gas upward and away from the fuel ahead, but wind leans them over the fuel, and then flame gas washes directly over it. The right-hand chart shows why radiation wins at flame temperatures: it grows as T⁴, convection only as T.

Conduction through a porous litter bed is real but slow. Ahead of a moving front its warm zone is a fraction of a millimetre long, so it barely registers.

There is an honest twist. Fine particles such as leaves and grass shed heat to the air so efficiently that radiation alone often cannot ignite them. Laboratory work by Finney et al. (2015, PNAS) found that ignition of fine fuels comes from direct flame contact, with radiation doing the preheating. Section 3 lets you test this.

3

Heating, drying, pyrolysis, ignition

Solid fuel does not burn. Heat breaks it down into flammable gas, and the gas burns. Follow one leaf as it is heated: it warms, stalls at 100 °C while its water boils off, then decomposes, and catches when the gas leaves its surface fast enough to sustain a flame.

An energy balance for one leaf

A leaf a third of a millimetre thick is thin enough that its temperature is the same all the way through. Its temperature then follows from bookkeeping. Absorbed radiation and any heat from hot gas come in; re-radiation and cooling by the air go out. What is left over raises the temperature, or boils water while there is any.

Pyrolysis follows the Arrhenius law. The rate climbs steeply with temperature: near 600 K, a 50 K rise multiplies it about forty times. That steepness is why ignition looks like a threshold.

4

From radiant heat to a rate of spread

Put the pieces together. A fire front moving at speed R must heat every kilogram of fuel ahead of it to ignition, and here the flame’s radiation is the only thing paying. Conservation of energy then fixes R.

The energy balance

Each square metre of ground holds w kg of fine fuel, and each kilogram needs Qig joules to dry it and bring it to ignition. A front advancing at R consumes R·w kg per second per metre of fire line, so it needs R·w·Qig watts. The radiant power the flame puts into the bed ahead, minus what the warming bed re-radiates and loses to the air, must supply exactly that.

With Flame sized by the fire switched on, flame length is no longer your input. A faster fire releases more energy and so grows a longer flame (Byram’s correlation), which radiates more and drives faster spread. The model iterates that loop to its fixed point.

Notice how weakly this rate responds to wind. Wind leans the flame and the radiation rises, but real wind-driven fires speed up far more. The missing physics is flame contact, turbulent gusts pushing flame into the fuel, and embers. Section 6 measures the gap against a real fire.

5

Spread across the Brindabellas

Operational models skip the physics and use relationships fitted to observed fires. This one runs McArthur’s Mk5 forest model over real terrain west of Canberra. Click the map to light a fire, set the weather, and play it forward.

Assumptions behind this output

Educational model only. Not an operational prediction.

How the map model works

Each cell’s arrival time is the quickest path from an ignition, where each step takes distance divided by the local rate of spread. That rate is McArthur’s Mk5 head-fire rate R = 0.0012 · FFDI · w, corrected for slope (doubling per 10° uphill) and shaped by an ellipse around the wind direction.

The ellipse’s length-to-breadth ratio now comes from a published relationship instead of a placeholder: Equation 79 of the Canadian Fire Behavior Prediction System, fitted to Alexander’s (1985) observations of forest fire shapes. CSIRO’s Spark framework points to the same source. At 46 km/h it gives 5.7. The Kilmore East fire was mapped at about 5 in that wind.

6

Checked against a real fire

The Black Saturday Kilmore East fire of 7 February 2009 was reconstructed hour by hour by CSIRO researchers (Cruz et al. 2012). Their published weather, fuel loads and measured spread rates make it a demanding test for any model.

What this model leaves out

  • No ember spotting, which carried the Kilmore East fire 30 km or more ahead of its front.
  • No fire–atmosphere coupling, no plume-driven winds, and no fire channelling in rugged terrain.
  • Weather is held constant for the whole run, and wind is uniform, unbent by hills.
  • Fuel is uniform and continuous. There are no roads, rivers, clearings or vegetation types.
  • Mk5 is known to under-predict summer wildfire spread by a factor of 2 to 3 (Burrows 1999; McCaw et al. 2008). CSIRO now recommends Vesta Mk 2 for dry eucalypt forest.
  • The physics sections idealise the flame as a flat panel of uniform temperature. Real flames flicker, vary in temperature and absorb their own radiation.
  • Terrain is an SRTM-derived ~62 m grid from AWS Terrain Tiles, not Geoscience Australia’s ELVIS data.

This is a teaching tool. It is not validated for operational use and must not inform evacuation, planning or firefighting decisions.
